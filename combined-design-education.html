Section 00

The whole chat, in plain words

The Feynman Technique: if you can’t explain something to a curious 12-year-old using only everyday words and analogies, you don’t actually understand it yet. Below is every part rebuilt that way — analogy first, then the plain-language mechanism, then why it matters.

The technique itself

1

Pick the concept

Take one idea from the chat, no matter how technical.

2

Teach it to a child

Explain it in the simplest words possible, with an everyday comparison.

3

Find the gap

Wherever you get stuck or reach for jargon, that’s what you don’t truly understand yet.

4

Simplify & reconnect

Go back, fill the gap, and rebuild the explanation until it flows without jargon.

This whole chat is really just two questions: “how do I think of a lot of ideas?” and “how do I find out — cheaply — which one is actually worth doing?”

Part 1 — The idea engine

Analogy

🎰 It’s like a fruit machine with four reels — instead of cherries and bells, the reels say WHO, WHAT-THEY-WANT, HOW, and WHO-PAYS.

You don’t sit and “think of a great idea.” You write 15 options on each reel, then spin combinations. Most combos are nonsense, but every so often one clicks — that’s a real business idea. Techniques like Pain Mining, SCAMPER, and Analogous Transplant are just different ways to fill in the reels so you’re not staring at a blank page.

Why it matters: most people get stuck waiting for one lightning-bolt idea instead of spinning the machine 100 times.

Part 2 — The idea bank (116 ideas)

Analogy

🎣 It’s like fishing with 11 different nets in 11 different parts of the lake, instead of casting one line where everyone else already fishes.

Bucket A is the obvious spot everyone fishes (generic design courses). The weird corners of the lake nobody tries — offline experiences, non-designer buyers, deliberately silly ideas — are where the good, uncrowded ideas turn up. You get 116 “fish” because you dragged a net through spots competitors never visit, not because you’re more creative than them.

Why it matters: skipping the weird corners is exactly where people leave the best ideas uncaught.

Part 3 — The reusable skill

Analogy

📖 It’s like turning a single good recipe into a cookbook — now anyone, including future-you, can cook a different meal with the same technique.

The design-education answer was one “meal.” The skill extracts the technique — the four-reel machine, the 11 fishing nets, the scoring checklist — so next time the domain is “pet care” or “logistics,” you don’t start from zero.

Why it matters: this is the difference between one good answer and a repeatable process forever.

Part 4 — Validation

Analogy

🌉 It’s like checking a bridge can hold weight with a calculator before you drive a truck across it, instead of finding out the hard way.

“People said they liked my idea” is like a bridge looking sturdy — it tells you nothing about the load it can bear. Napkin math, CAC/LTV, and the payment-link test are the calculator: cheap, boring, done before you spend real time or money. Interviews check whether people want to cross the bridge; money tests check whether they’ll actually pay the toll.

Why it matters: most failed businesses didn’t fail because nobody wanted them — the math never worked, and nobody checked it first.

The golden thread

The one sentence that connects everything

💰 “Who pays, and how much, and how easily can you find more of them?” — that single question does more work than any clever idea.

Notice this shows up everywhere: the payer-swap trick in Part 1, the “sell to employers not students” pattern in Part 2’s idea bank, and the entire point of Part 4’s napkin math. It’s the same insight wearing three different outfits.

Self-test (the actual Feynman gap check)

Try explaining “the payer swap” out loud to someone, in one breath, with no jargon. If you hesitate between “who pays” and “who benefits,” that’s the part to re-read in Part 1 — that’s your real knowledge gap, not a memory problem.

Section 01

Why “just think of a good idea” doesn’t work — and what to do instead

Nobody comes up with a whole business idea in a single flash of inspiration. What actually works is breaking the problem into small, separate pieces, combining those pieces in lots of different ways, and being okay with the fact that most combinations will be bad. The good ideas only show up once you’ve generated enough bad ones to get past the obvious answers.

The core engine — how we broke the problem down
WHOthe segment
× OUTCOMEthe job-to-be-done
× HOWthe format
× MONEYwho pays, and when

Write 15 honest answers to each of these four questions and you can combine them roughly 50,000 different ways. Most combinations are nonsense. Around 50 are genuinely interesting. Maybe 3 are worth pursuing. The point isn’t to admire all 50,000 — it’s to generate enough raw material that a few good ones are guaranteed to be hiding in there.

Four families of thinking technique

We pulled together 52 different “thinking tools” and grouped them by what kind of thinking each one triggers.

Group 1

Finding real pain people already have

Reading complaints, watching bad workarounds, asking what someone actually did the last time something went wrong. Don’t invent a problem — go find one that already exists.

Includes Pain Mining and Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD).
Group 2

Taking an industry apart to find the gaps

Every industry bundles several things into one product. Pull the bundle apart — offer one piece really well, or combine pieces nobody's thought to combine.

Includes Unbundling, Rebundling, and the Before / During / After lens.
Group 3

Spotting price and value gaps

The exact same value is often priced very differently depending on where, how, or to whom it's sold — cheap in one place, expensive in another; free until a law makes it mandatory.

Known as Arbitrage thinking, including Time Arbitrage — spotting what's "newly true."
Group 4

Looking at what you already know

Sometimes the best idea is something you already understand better than most people, or something people already ask you for help with.

Also called a Founder-Market Fit inventory.

A few techniques, explained with the design-education example

1

Look at the “before” and “after,” not just the main thing

Nearly every design course competes on the same moment — the weeks of learning itself. But there's a stretch before someone signs up (deciding which career fits them) and a stretch after (portfolio, interviews, the first three months, eventual promotion). Almost nobody serves those two stretches.

This turned out to be the single most useful lens in the entire brainstorm. Technique name: Before / During / After Sequencing.

2

Ask who else could pay, besides the obvious person

Keep the exact same product and change who's expected to pay. Make it free for a junior designer, and charge the employer who badly needs trained designers instead. Nothing about the teaching changes — you're just charging someone with a real budget.

Probably the single most valuable reframe in this whole conversation: "who else benefits, and do they have more money?"

3

Borrow structures from completely different industries

Gyms have drop-in classes and memberships, music schools have graded exams, martial arts schools have belts and lineage from teacher to student. What would the design-world version of a dojo actually look like?

Technique name: Analogous Transplant.

4

Watch for new laws that force people to spend money

When a regulation makes something mandatory, the affected company has no choice but to pay for a fix. A recent EU law made web accessibility a legal requirement — accessibility training suddenly has employers with a real, unavoidable, budgeted need.

Technique name: Regulatory / Mandate Arbitrage.

5

Imagine the worst possible version, then do the opposite

Picture the worst design school imaginable and list everything wrong with it — no feedback, no other students, videos you just watch passively. Check how many existing schools are quietly guilty of each item, then flip every flaw into a strength.

The prompt was literally: "what would the worst design school look like?" Technique name: Inversion.

6

Systematically change one part of the standard offering

A checklist of seven small changes you can force onto any idea — substitute a piece, combine it with something else, remove a part entirely, or reverse how it normally works. Removing and reversing tend to produce the most surprising results.

Applying "remove something" produced ideas like "a school with no teachers" or "a course with no videos." Technique name: SCAMPER.

7

Start from who you can already reach, not from the product

Most people design the perfect product first and only think about finding customers afterwards. It works better in reverse: figure out which group of people you can realistically get in front of for free, right now, and design something specifically for them.

The question to keep asking: "whose attention can I get cheaply, and what would they actually buy?" Technique name: Distribution-First Ideation.

Section 02

Putting the engine to work: 116 real ideas for a design-education business

Once we had the four-question engine and the 52 thinking techniques, we used them on your specific situation. Rather than brainstorming loosely, we split the search into 8 clearly different buckets, each aimed at a different part of the market — because the obvious bucket (generic design courses) is the one everyone else has already crowded into.

A handful of the strongest ideas that came out of this exercise

#64
An apprenticeship placement agency

Training is free for the student — the employer pays a fee once they hire. This completely removes the risk for the person you're trying to help.

#3
Teaching people to design for AI products

A genuinely new skill (how AI tools should behave, designing around uncertainty) that barely anyone teaches yet — no established competitor to beat.

#45
"The Design Gym" — a membership, not a course

Modelled on how gyms work: people pay monthly to keep showing up and practising, so you're not stuck constantly creating brand-new content.

#99
Helping Indian designers land international clients

Same exact skill, sold to a market that pays roughly five times as much — the single biggest pure "pay gap" we found.

#32
A sales bootcamp specifically for designers

The sharper version of your original instinct. The real problem most designers have isn't "freelancing" — it's that they don't know how to sell.

#49
Crit Club — a paid monthly critique dinner

People pay to get their work reviewed over dinner once a month. Cheap to run, high margin, and it naturally builds a loyal community.

The pattern we noticed once we stepped back

Four out of the five strongest ideas above don't rely on a broke junior designer being the one who pays. Instead the money comes from an employer, a college, a company, or a wealthier overseas market. That points to the biggest mistake almost everyone in design education makes: assuming the student has to be the customer, when the student usually has the least money in the whole chain.

Section 03

Turning this whole method into a tool you can reuse for any future idea

Once we'd built and used this process for design education, it seemed wasteful to only use it once. So the next step was to strip out everything specific to "design education" and rebuild the method as a reusable tool — something you can point at any domain in the future and get the same structured brainstorm back. Think of it as turning one good recipe into a general cookbook technique.

~/.claude/skills/business-brainstorm/
├── SKILL.md                 the overall instructions: how to run the process, and in what order
└── references/
    ├── frameworks.md        all 52 thinking techniques, organized into their 8 groups
    ├── divergence-axes.md   the four-question engine, plus the 11 buckets to sort ideas into
    ├── convergence.md       how to score ideas and decide which ones to actually test
    └── output-template.md   the format the final write-up should follow

Three ways to run it, depending on how much depth you need

"Full" mode

110–150 ideas

The complete process, exactly like design education. Use this when doing serious research on a new business direction.

"Quick" mode

Around 40 ideas

A faster pass that still covers the main techniques and gets you to a shortlist of the top 3. Good for a fast mid-conversation check.

"Deep" mode

1–5 ideas, in depth

Takes ideas you've already picked and builds a full case: market size, competitors, pricing, and a 30-day test plan.

Rules built into the tool, so quality never slips

It must always produce at least 110 ideas in full mode, every idea must clearly name who it's for and what outcome it delivers, at least 10 ideas must not depend entirely on the founder doing all the work, and at least 8 must involve someone other than the end user paying the bill. It's also required to step back and name the patterns it noticed, not just list ideas.

Section 04

Having an idea people like isn't the same as having a real business

This was the most important correction in the whole conversation. Everything up to this point answered one question: "will people want this?" But that's one of four things you need to know. The other three — does the money work, can you deliver it, will it still work in three years — get skipped by almost everyone, even though the money question is the cheapest to check.

Gate 1

Do people want it?

The one question almost everyone remembers — usually asked via friends or a few interviews.

Gate 2

Does the math work?

Can you charge enough, to enough people, to make a living? Free to check, most often skipped.

Gate 3

Can you deliver it?

Do you have the time, skill, and money to produce this at the price you're charging?

Gate 4

Will it still work in 3 years?

Or will it get copied, made obsolete, or squeezed out the moment it works?

A simple back-of-the-envelope calculation that can save you months

This kind of quick math is often called a Napkin Test; building it up from real numbers rather than a vague market size is called Bottom-Up estimating.

OPTION A — Selling directly to individual students, ₹40,000 per seat
To make ₹1 crore a year, you need:
  1,00,00,000 ÷ 40,000                    = 250 students across the year
  250 students ÷ 4 batches a year         = 62 students per batch
  62 ÷ 3% of applicants who actually pay  = ~2,100 applications needed
  2,100 ÷ 2% of your audience who applies = ~1,05,000 people need to see you
→ You'd need an audience of roughly 100,000 people. Very hard to build alone in year one.

OPTION B — Selling to companies instead, ₹5,00,000 per contract
To make the same ₹1 crore a year, you need:
  1,00,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000                        = 20 companies signed up
  20 ÷ 25% of conversations that convert        = 80 real sales conversations
  80 ÷ 15% of cold outreach that gets a meeting = ~530 outreach messages, ~11/week
→ One person, working alone, can realistically do this in year one.

Nothing about the actual teaching changed between these two options — only who you're selling to and how much you're charging. This is why it's worth doing this math before talking to customers: Option A could pass twenty encouraging interviews and still be a business you can't build.

Numbers worth knowing when checking if a business makes financial sense

3x or higher

a customer should be worth at least 3× what it costs to acquire them (LTV:CAC) — below 2× you're losing money to grow

Under 3 months

a healthy CAC payback period if you're self-funding without outside investors

40–65%

a healthy profit margin on live, cohort-based teaching programs

Under 2%/mo

a healthy churn rate for a business selling to other companies

A practical 6-week plan for comparing several ideas

Week 1
All ideas

Napkin math + bottom-up sizing + Five Forces

Costs nothing but time. Usually eliminates 2 of 5 ideas immediately, just from the numbers not adding up.

Weeks 2–3
Top 3

Competitor research + ~10 real customer conversations each

Check competitors' job postings, their running ads (an Ad Library search shows exactly what they're spending), and public reviews (Review Mining). In India, MCA/ROC filings can even show a competitor's real revenue. Usually removes one more idea.

Weeks 4–5
Top 2

Try to actually get people to pay, for both at once

Set up a real way to pay before the product fully exists, and let real behaviour decide which idea wins.

Week 6
Winner

Deliver the service by hand to five real people

Fully manual, no polished product yet — this is often called a Concierge test. You'll learn more here than in six months of planning.

How to rule an idea out before you've even talked to anyone

If you're comparing several ideas side by side, score each one on a simple Scorecard and write your Kill Criteria in advance — the exact number that, if you don't hit it, means you agree to walk away.

Costs nothing

Find the one assumption that would sink everything

Ask: "which single belief, if wrong, makes this whole idea pointless?" Test only that, for the least money possible.

Technique name: the Riskiest Assumption Test.
Costs nothing

Sort your assumptions by how risky they are

Rate each assumption on importance and evidence. Only high-importance, low-evidence assumptions deserve attention right now.

Technique name: Assumption Mapping.
About the market

Check if the whole market is structurally difficult

Too many competitors, easy switching, powerful suppliers — sometimes the market itself is a hard place to make money, no matter how well you execute.

Technique name: Porter's Five Forces.
Long-term thinking

Ask what gets stronger over time

Some businesses compound — brand, data, community. Others start from scratch every year. Know honestly which kind yours is.

Technique name: the 7 Powers framework (Hamilton Helmer).

Three financial terms worth understanding, explained simply

"Break-even" — the number of sales you need before you stop losing money overall.
   Calculated as: your fixed costs, divided by the profit you make per sale.

"Contribution margin" — the actual profit left over from one sale,
   after subtracting only the costs that rise when you serve one more customer.
   If negative, more customers means more losses, not less.

"Payback period" — how many months it takes for one customer to earn back
   what it cost to acquire them. If you're self-funding, this matters more than
   almost anything else — it's about your cash running out, not eventual profit.

Four practical ways to figure out what to charge

Method

Try three prices at once

Show different groups the same offer at three prices and see which converts best.

Also called a Price Ladder test.
Method

Ask at rising price points

Find the exact point where most people suddenly say no — that's your ceiling.

Also called the Gabor-Granger method.
Method

Ask four framing questions

Too expensive / worth it / a bargain / suspiciously cheap — combine the answers into a sensible price range.

Also called the Van Westendorp method.
Method

Just say a real number out loud

On a real call, quote your real price and watch the reaction. Costs nothing, tells you more than most surveys.

Sometimes nicknamed the Wince Test.

Proving you can deliver, before building anything at all

The delivery ladder — each step costs ~10× the one before it
1 · Smoke testput up a simple page and see if anyone shows real interest
2 · Conciergedeliver the whole thing yourself, by hand, with no shortcuts
3 · Wizard of Ozmake it look automated while you secretly do it by hand behind the scenes
4 · Real MVPbuild one real feature, only once earlier steps proved people will pay

How to talk to people, and what comes after picking a winner

Interviewing

Ask about the past, not the future

Never ask "would you buy this?" Ask what they actually did the last time this exact problem came up. Past behaviour is a far more honest signal than an imagined future.

Often called The Mom Test, built on asking about past behaviour.
Managing risk

Imagine it's already failed, and work backwards

Picture it's 18 months from now and the business failed — write exactly why. Check which causes you could test cheaply this month.

Technique name: a Pre-Mortem.
What comes next

Picking the idea is only half the decision

Once you've chosen what to build, there's a second decision left: how to deliver it. Skipping this is how a good idea quietly becomes a mediocre business.

This second round of choices is called The Second Diamond.
So what does "properly validated" actually mean?

Somewhere between 5 and 10 complete strangers — not friends or family — have handed over real money for something that doesn't even fully exist yet. You know roughly what it cost to find each of them, and you can already name the next 50 people you'd go after. Short of that, what you have is polite interest, not proof.

Section 05

What connects all four parts, once you look at them together

If you only read each part on its own, you'd miss a few patterns that only become visible once you look at the whole conversation as one continuous story.

Pattern 1

"Who actually pays" mattered more than almost anything else

The "who else could pay" reframe, the strongest ideas in the bank, and the napkin-math comparison all point to the same truth: the exact same skill, effort, and person can produce a business ten times more viable, just by changing who's expected to pay. Across this entire conversation, that one decision mattered more than idea quality, format, or even market size.

Pattern 2

Coming up with ideas and testing them are really the same skill, in reverse

In the first two parts, we generated as many ideas as possible without judging any of them early. In the fourth part, we did almost the opposite — found the fastest, cheapest way to prove an idea wrong. If you only generate without testing, nothing gets built. If you only test without first generating widely, you never find the genuinely interesting idea.

Pattern 3

The same trap — focusing only on the "middle" — showed up again and again

Design courses focus on the middle of the learning journey. Most validation efforts focus on the middle question too — "do people want this?" — while skipping "does the math work" and "will this matter in three years." Before/during/after isn't just for finding course ideas — it's a general way to notice where any crowded market has left gaps.

Pattern 4

The cheapest tests are usually the ones people skip

Rough market-sizing math, checking job postings, reading public reviews — none of it costs money, and yet almost nobody does it before diving into interviews or building something. The biggest shift in how we approached validation: do the free, boring arithmetic first, and only spend real time talking to customers once an idea survives that cheap filter.

The one thing worth remembering above everything else

Your original problem — "I know the domain, but I don't know what to build" — was never about a lack of creativity. It was about not having a clear process: no structured way to generate a lot of ideas, and no structured way to cheaply rule out the weak ones before getting attached to them. Everything in this conversation exists to give you that missing process, not to hand you one single "correct" answer.

Section 06

Out of everything we discussed, here's what stands out as genuinely worth pursuing

Not ranked by which idea sounds most exciting — ranked by real structural advantages: good timing, someone with money paying for it, protection from competitors, and an easy way to reach customers.

1

An apprenticeship or "train-then-place" model

The student pays nothing; the employer pays a fee once you've placed a trained designer with them. Hardest to set up of all these ideas, but structurally the strongest — it removes the hardest part, convincing an individual to pay upfront.

2

Teaching design specifically for AI products

A genuinely new area of design with no well-established competitor yet. This kind of open window doesn't stay open forever — usually only a year or two — so there's real value in moving while it's still wide open.

3

An offline design "gym" or a monthly critique club

Probably the easiest of these ideas to actually start. No constant new content required, real loyalty because people show up in person, and startable next month with twenty people and a rented room.

4

Helping Indian designers find international clients

The single biggest pure pay gap found in the whole conversation: identical skills, roughly five times the pay, purely from which market you sell into and how you position yourself.

5

A sales training program built specifically for designers

The sharper, more useful version of your original instinct to "teach designers to run a business." Generic freelancing advice is everywhere — almost nobody addresses the one skill designers actually lack: knowing how to sell.

★

And don't overlook the tool itself — the reusable business-brainstorm skill

Valuable beyond this one decision — point it at any future business idea, not just design education. A natural next step: a companion validation-focused tool carrying over the napkin-math methods, competitor research approach, and the six-week testing plan from Part 4.

Section 01

How the thinking moved

Five stages. Each one narrowed the problem rather than adding to it — which is the actual work of early strategy.

1

From brainstorming to conviction

Volume of ideas is the wrong goal.

The opening problem was framed as “I can’t brainstorm.” It was re-diagnosed as generating at the wrong altitude: “education + design” is a category, not an idea. The fix was to swap idea-volume for a directional hypothesis — one sentence stating what design education wrongly assumes and what should replace it — then sharpen it through criticism rather than agreement.

2

Mapping the whole field

You cannot pick a position without a map.

Twelve families of education business, from degree-granting institutions to exam coaching, cohorts, self-paced marketplaces, creator media, communities, 1:1 coaching, employer-funded training, embodied learning, games, AI-native tools, and infrastructure. Each with its own payer, margin, and 2026 trajectory.

3

A decision method, not a decision

Eliminate on paper. Decide in the market.

Ideas written as full sentences; hard constraints used to delete rather than optimise; ten real buyer conversations per surviving idea; then pick whichever came alive. The key discrimination: real interest costs the buyer something — money, a calendar slot, their reputation in a referral. Politeness is free.

4

Generating the actual candidates

Ideas are downstream of problems.

Six generation methods produced twenty-six specific ideas across six clusters — from showing the hidden mess of real design work, to teaching judgment rather than execution, to serving the abandoned majority of designers who work in banks, hospitals and internal tools.

5

The constraint that resolved it

“I want to teach, not run a media company.”

A stated preference removed roughly a third of the list — the content archives, licensing plays and placement guilds are publishing and service businesses, not teaching ones. What survived was a shortlist where the founder’s live voice is the product, and one clear recommendation emerged from it.

Section 03

Six ways to generate ideas that aren’t obvious

The point of writing these down is repeatability. A founder who owns the generator never needs to be handed ideas again.

Method 01

Flip the “why” — innovation of meaning

Don’t ask how to teach design better. Ask what design education is for, and whether that is still true. Old answer: teach people to make things. If machines now make things, the new answer is: teach people to judge things. A different why produces a different school.

Arrived at through criticism, not ideation: write the hypothesis alone, break it with one sparring partner, harden it with a small radical circle, then compare interpretations with outsiders.

Method 02

Hunt what the field hides

Every profession has a highlight reel and a hidden reality. Design shows only finished, polished work — never the forty rejected versions, the argument that killed the good idea, or the compromise with engineering. Whatever a field hides is a market.

Method 03

Find the abandoned people

Ask who has the problem but has nobody serving them. Not the fashionable segment — the ignored one. In design that means enterprise, government, healthcare, internal tools, and the solo designer with no team.

Method 04

Unbundle, then rebundle

A course is five things glued together: content, deadlines, feedback, peers, certificate. Sell one of them separately and better. Feedback alone becomes a critique subscription. The certificate alone becomes the highest-margin product on the map.

Method 05

Find the broken step

In every career path there is one rung where everyone gets stuck and nobody teaches the climb. In design: senior to staff, individual contributor to manager, and — newly — junior to employed at all.

Method 06

Mutate the model, not the content

Doblin’s ten types of innovation, used as mutations. Keep the teaching identical and change only the money, only the partners, only the process, or only who pays. The empirical finding worth remembering: businesses stacking five or more types outperform, and most companies overinvest in the offering — the easiest thing to copy.

Section 04

The twelve families of education business

Everything anyone has ever been taught by, sorted by who pays and what actually gets sold. Read it to locate white space, not to admire the breadth.

Family 01

Formal institutional

Universities, K-12, alternative schooling (Montessori, Waldorf, Reggio, democratic), microschools and pods, vocational and polytechnic, community colleges, professional schools, online-degree providers, university executive education.

Sells: credential and access. Payer: family, state, employer.

Protected by an accreditation monopoly, rotting on value for money. Microschools are the fast-growing edge: small, physical, high-trust, low capex. Entering as a school is a decade-long capital play; entering as a supplier to schools starts next month.

Family 02

Exam & credential prep

Entrance coaching (JEE, NEET, UPSC), standardised tests, shadow tutoring (juku, hagwon, tuition centres), professional certification (CFA, CPA, PMP, AWS), language exams.

Sells: a number on a page — unambiguous, which is why it monetises so well. Payer: parents, the highest willingness-to-pay group in existence.

The most reliably profitable category in education, recurring by demography. AI threatens the explanation layer, not the pressure layer — parents buy discipline and peer competition, not content.

Family 03

Cohorts & bootcamps

Coding and design bootcamps, cohort-based courses (Maven, Reforge, On Deck), accelerators teaching in exchange for equity, fellowships, immersive residencies, hire-train-deploy.

Sells: accountability, network, claimed outcome. Payer: learner, increasingly employer.

Completion runs 70–90% against under 15% for self-paced; that gap is the product. The shakeout was real — Dev Bootcamp, Launch Academy, Codeup, Epicodus all closed. The lesson isn’t that cohorts fail; it’s that anyone promising “become a junior developer” died when junior work automated and hiring froze.

Family 04

Self-paced digital

Marketplaces (Udemy, Skillshare, Domestika), MOOCs, subscription libraries (Pluralsight, MasterClass, O’Reilly), single-creator info products, practice platforms (Duolingo, Brilliant, DataCamp), vendor certifications, microlearning.

Sells: information — which is the problem.

Infinite margin, near-zero pricing power, dismal completion. The most directly disrupted category on the map. Exception: practice-and-feedback products survive, because the product is the exercise loop, not the explanation. Never sell recorded video as the primary product.

Family 05

Creator & media-led

YouTube, podcasts, newsletters, short-form video, books, open documentation, live-streamed building, documentary.

Sells: free attention at the front, anything else behind it.

A marketing channel disguised as a product. Ad revenue is a rounding error; the audience is the asset. Still the highest-leverage start for someone with no capital and no brand — and as AI floods every feed, a specific human with a real point of view becomes more valuable, not less.

Family 06

Community & membership

Paid communities, masterminds and peer advisory boards, professional associations with mandatory CPD credits, conferences and unconferences, competitions, open-source contribution as apprenticeship.

Sells: access, purely.

Recurring revenue, margin improving with scale, and the members create the value while you curate — the highest defensibility on the map, because a network takes years to reproduce. Brutal cold-start. Overlooked goldmine: legally mandated continuing-education credits for doctors, accountants, architects and engineers.

Family 07

One-to-one high touch

Private tutoring and marketplaces (Preply, Wyzant, Superprof), executive and career coaching, mentorship platforms, advisory retainers, atelier critique, sports coaching.

Sells: judgment, and being seen.

Highest price per hour, worst leverage — you are selling your calendar. Protected against AI relationally, exposed informationally. The fastest way to learn a market and earn money; a terrible place to remain.

Family 08

Employer-paid & B2B

Corporate L&D and LMS, corporate universities, per-seat upskilling, compliance training, leadership and sales training firms, enablement, consultancy academies.

Sells: risk reduction and capability — bought by someone who is not the learner.

The strongest demand signal on the map. Companies are urgently retraining for AI and don’t know how. Contracts run from tens to hundreds of thousands. One contract can equal a year of consumer course sales at a fraction of the marketing cost.

Family 09

Experiential & embodied

Apprenticeship and guild systems, the studio/atelier model, simulation and VR training, emergency and military drills, outdoor education, museums, makerspaces, culinary schools, residencies, field schools, language immersion.

Sells: an experience that cannot travel through a screen.

Capital-heavy, geographically bound, exceptional word of mouth. Structurally immune to AI — which is the whole thesis. As digital learning becomes infinite and free, in-person scarcity becomes a luxury good.

Family 10

Play & games

Educational and gamified apps, business simulations, toy-based learning, roleplay formats — debate, Model UN, moot court.

Sells: engagement and repetition, disguised so the learner stops resisting.

Expensive to build well, brutal retention maths, enormous when it lands. Generative AI collapses content-production cost, moving the constraint from asset creation to game design.

Family 11

AI-native & adaptive

AI tutors, adaptive assessment, automated feedback and grading, role-play simulators for sales calls and clinical interviews, teacher-productivity tools.

Around 2,800 AI-focused education startups in 2026 against roughly a tenth of that in 2023; AI tutoring reportedly reaching tens of millions of learners. Also the most crowded and capital-intensive corner of the map — the dimension where a solo founder has least advantage. The asymmetric play is not to build the AI but to supply the layer it cannot: accountability, judgment, network, credential.

Family 12

Infrastructure & enablement

Course platforms and LMS, assessment and credentialing bodies, curriculum licensing and white-label, textbook publishing, franchising (Kumon, Berlitz, Sylvan), income-share financiers, marketing-funded academies used as software funnels.

Sells: to educators, not learners.

The best economics in the sector — you capture value from everyone’s teaching while doing none. Licensing and franchising are chronically under-considered by individual founders. Kumon is a franchise, not a tutoring business; that is the entire trick.

Section 06

How to decide, in four steps

You cannot think your way to this answer, because you don’t have the information yet. So: eliminate on paper, decide in the market.

1

Write it as a sentence

A topic is not an idea.

State who it’s for, what they get, and what it costs. “I’ll teach 12 design students to build a portfolio, over six weeks online, for ₹15,000 each.” Now it can be judged. Do this for three or four ideas so they’re comparable.

2

Delete what you can’t start

One bad answer and the idea is crossed out — don’t repair it.

  • Can I name twenty real buyers today, without paid advertising?
  • Do they need this now, or is it a “someday I should”?
  • Will money come in within six months?
  • Am I proud to say it out loud — several hundred times?

Four ideas usually become two here. That is the function, not a failure.

3

Talk to ten people per surviving idea

Never pitch. Ask about their past.

“Last time you tried to fix this, what did you do?” · “Did you pay for anything? How much?” · “What was annoying about it?” You are testing whether the problem is real enough that they have already spent time or money on it. Real buyers only — friends and family don’t count.

4

Pick the one that came alive

Real interest costs them something. Politeness is free.

They really want it

They ask the price unprompted · they block a date · they pay something · they refer a friend · they describe the pain in detail you never asked for

Just being polite

“Great idea!” · “You should definitely do it.” · “Send me the details when it’s ready.” — this one especially means no

If neither came alive, that is the most valuable outcome available: two weeks spent instead of two years. Change the hypothesis and repeat.

Weighting, when two survive

Score one to five. The weights are the part most founders get backwards.

CriterionWeightThe real question
Can you reach the buyer?×3Can you name twenty specific people or companies today?
Is the problem on fire?×3Is it hurting them this quarter, or theoretically?
Does a budget already exist?×2Are they already spending on this line item, even badly?
Cost to test×2Can you get a real yes or no cheaply, in three weeks?
Unfair advantage×1Credibility, network, or insight others lack
Ceiling×1Could this be large in three years, if it works?

Reach and urgency outweigh everything else combined. Market size — the criterion that feels most important — matters least at this stage, because a large market you cannot reach is worth exactly nothing.

Three traps

Picking what excites you most. Excitement is information about you, not about demand. Use it only to break a tie between options that already passed.

Picking the biggest market. Big markets mean big competitors and expensive attention.

Picking what you’re best at building. The most seductive one — building is comfortable, selling is exposing. It’s how people end up with a beautiful course nobody wants.

Section 09

The recommendation: teach what happens after the design is finished

Working title: The Real Work. A live cohort, twelve people, five weeks, online. Chosen because it is the direct expression of the founder’s own conviction — and the one thing schools structurally cannot teach.

School ends when the design is done. Real work starts there.

Week 1
The brief is vague and keeps changing. Work anyway.
Week 2
Present to a hostile stakeholder. Get torn apart. Do it again.
Week 3
Engineering says no. Negotiate what you’ll cut.
Week 4
Scope and price the work honestly.
Week 5
Your best idea got killed. Ship the compromise with your standards intact.

How it’s taught

Not lectures — role-play. You play the difficult VP. You play the engineer who says it’s impossible. You play the client who won’t pay. Students present, you push back hard, the group watches, everyone dissects it, then they go again. The pedagogy is the differentiation: this content cannot be delivered by a recording, a PDF, or a language model, because the value is in the pressure.

Why this one, against the alternatives

  • It is teaching. Your live voice every week is the product, which was the stated constraint.
  • Nobody is doing it. Everyone teaches design. Almost nobody teaches surviving design.
  • No capital, no platform, no product. A video call and twelve people. Testable inside six weeks.
  • It has an obvious escalation. Once it works on individuals, companies will pay many times more for the same five weeks delivered to their design team — which moves you from the worst economic shape to the best.
  • The pain is immediate. A designer who was destroyed in a review yesterday will buy this today. Urgency, which carries the heaviest weight in the scoring.
Runner-up

Designing AI Products. The highest demand on the entire map and genuinely no curriculum exists — but only viable with real hands-on experience shipping AI features. It cannot be taught from reading, and students detect borrowed authority immediately.

The growth sequence

Then grow Small group→ Polish it→ Sell to companies→ Build a community

Companies pay far better than individuals, but no company will buy until you can show it already worked on real people. And the community only functions once there are alumni to seed it. The order is not optional.

Section 10

Ten insights worth keeping after the details fade

The transferable part. These outlive whichever idea gets launched first.

Insight 01

Your three abandoned ideas were one idea in three costumes

Bridging bootcamps and industry, splitting cohorts by B2B versus B2C, and going experiential were not three attempts — they were one belief circling for a name: design education teaches the clean version of a messy job.

Repeated returns to the same territory are the strongest evidence a founder has of genuine conviction, and it is almost always discarded as indecision. Treat the pattern in your discarded ideas as data, not noise.

Insight 02

Information is now worth zero; the other four goods are worth more than ever

Of the five things education can sell — information, accountability, access, credential, judgment — only the first was destroyed by generative AI. The other four became scarcer by contrast. Every viable 2026 education business is a machine for delivering one of the remaining four, using content merely as bait.

Insight 03

AI removed the bottom rung of the career ladder, and nobody has replaced it

Junior work was how the profession trained itself: cheap labour in exchange for proximity to masters. Automate the cheap labour and the training mechanism disappears with it — which is a structural hole, not a market cycle.

This is why the bootcamp shakeout happened, and why “become a junior” collapsed as a promise while “become someone with senior judgment” did not. Whoever rebuilds apprenticeship for this era owns a decade.

Insight 04

Completion rate is the product, not a metric

Self-paced courses complete under 15%. Cohorts complete 70–90%. That gap is the entire value proposition of live teaching, and it is also the whole marketing story. Enrolment is vanity; finishing is the thing people are actually failing at.

Insight 05

In education, glamour and profit are inversely correlated

Compliance training, mandatory continuing-education credits, enterprise design, internal tools, exam coaching — unglamorous, near-guaranteed demand, little competition. Consumer courses, creator education and AI tutors — exciting, crowded, brutal.

The discomfort you feel toward a boring category is a rough measure of how uncontested it is.

Insight 06

Changing who pays is the largest single lever available

The same five weeks of teaching is worth a modest fee from an individual and many times that from their employer. No change to the content, the curriculum, or the effort — only the payer. Founders spend months improving the offering while ignoring the one variable with an order-of-magnitude effect.

Insight 07

Scarcity has inverted, and premium pricing will follow it

Digital, reproducible learning is heading to free. Embodied, located, in-person, capacity-limited learning is heading toward luxury. Apprenticeships, studios, residencies, and small physical cohorts are not nostalgic — they are the categories AI cannot enter.

Insight 08

Choosing to teach is choosing speed over scale — so sequence deliberately

Teaching is the worst economic shape on the map and the best learning instrument. That is a fair trade, provided it is temporary. Individuals to learn, companies to earn, community to compound. A teacher who never graduates from selling hours has bought a job, not built a business.

Insight 09

Whatever a profession hides is where its market sits

Design’s entire visible corpus is survivorship-biased: finished work, polished cases, successful launches. The rejected versions, the killed ideas, the political defeats and the eighteen-month aftermath are systematically invisible — which is precisely why practitioners are unprepared for them.

Ask of any field: what does everyone experience and nobody publish?

Insight 10

Choose a promise that is arithmetic

“Learn to price your work” sells itself because the buyer can compute the payback before paying. “Become a better designer” cannot be computed and therefore has to be sold. Where two ideas are otherwise equal, take the one whose return the buyer can calculate in their head.

Section 12

What could kill this

Stated plainly, because the failure modes here are predictable and mostly self-inflicted.

Borrowed authority

Teaching “how real work goes” without having survived it. Students detect this in one session, and the reputational damage is permanent in a small field. Two honest alternatives: host and bring in practitioners as the difficult stakeholders, or teach from genuine strength.

Building before selling

The most seductive failure. Curriculum is comfortable to make and content is now the cheapest input available. Nothing should be built before the first cohort is paid for.

Free students

They don’t finish, don’t complain usefully, and teach you nothing about willingness to pay. Price low if necessary, but price.

Staying in the hours business

High-touch teaching produces cash and a ceiling simultaneously. Without a deliberate move to employers or recurring revenue, this becomes a demanding self-employment rather than a company.

Confusing enrolment with success

A full first cohort that finishes at 30% is a failure wearing the costume of a win. Completion and outcome are the only honest measures.

Choosing the interesting idea

Interesting and sellable are different properties. The elimination questions exist specifically to override taste, and they should be allowed to.

Section 13

The next six weeks

Everything above resolves to a small amount of unglamorous activity.

Week 1
Write the hypothesis in one sentence. Turn three ideas into full sentences — who, what, how much. Run the four elimination questions.
Week 2
Assemble three to five people who share your irritation with how design is taught. One session to argue it into a single direction.
Weeks 3–4
Twenty problem interviews with real buyers. No pitching. In parallel, force five or more Doblin types into non-obvious positions and write the model on one page.
Week 5
Teach one ninety-minute session, free, to five real people. Watch precisely where attention dies.
Week 6
Pre-sell. Real price, real payment link, nothing built. Ten paid from roughly a hundred qualified conversations is a pass.

Validation thresholds, set before starting

StageActionPass threshold
0Twenty interviews, never pitchingTwelve or more describe the same specific pain unprompted
1One free ninety-minute session for five peopleFour show up; two ask when the next one is and whether they can pay
2Pre-sell with a live payment linkTen paid from about a hundred qualified conversations

Section 14

The one question still open

Everything else in this brief is settled enough to act on. This isn’t.

Three or more years in a real team with real stakeholders, and you teach The Real Work from lived experience. Mostly academic so far, and there are two honest routes instead: host rather than teach — you facilitate and practitioners play the hostile VP, the engineer, the client — or teach from genuine strength, which for a strategy and innovation background means design strategy, problem framing, and the business of design. The idea does not change. The credibility structure does.
